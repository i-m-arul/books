The guards changed at 8:17 a.m.

It was a minor deviation—seven minutes earlier than the time printed on the briefing sheet folded inside the man’s jacket. Seven minutes that did not belong to this day. Seven minutes that had never existed before.

He stood across the road from the Prime Minister’s residence on Safdarjung Road, appearing to study the slow choreography of traffic. An Ambassador car idled too long at the curb. A cyclist hesitated, then crossed. Delhi was already warm, the heat settling at the back of the neck and refusing to move.

He did not look at his watch.

He didn’t need to.

He knew the moment she would step outside.
He knew the sari.
He knew where the cameras would be waiting.

What he remembered most clearly was not the sound of the shots, but the absence that preceded them—the brief, unnatural stillness, as if the world had drawn a breath and forgotten to release it.

The guards were early.

Subtle changes were always the most dangerous. Loud ones announced themselves. Subtle ones crept.

He shifted his weight and let his gaze settle on the gate. Two men took their positions. One adjusted his turban with practiced efficiency. The other stood very still, eyes forward, expression sealed.

In the version of the day he carried with him, one of them would have been late.

That delay—thirty-four seconds, caused by a conversation about tea—had mattered.

This time, there had been no conversation.

The tightening behind his eyes was not panic. It was calculation.

It’s already moving.

He crossed the road and walked past the gate, close enough to feel the quiet authority of the place. The residence looked smaller in the morning light, less fortress than habit. Power here was not architectural. It was assumed.

Inside the compound, a gardener knelt by a hedge, trimming patiently. Somewhere deeper within, a telephone rang and stopped.

He paused near a neem tree and pretended to light a cigarette he would not smoke. His hands were steady. That had surprised him the first time. He had expected some betrayal—sweat, tremor, a visible fracture. Instead, his body behaved as if this were routine. Another assignment. Another file refusing to stay closed.

He had told himself this was about correction.

Negligence. Oversight. A chain of small, human failures later dressed up as destiny. If procedures had been followed as written—if one man had been reassigned, if another had been removed—the day would have ended differently.

That belief had carried him through the echo.

Across the road, the guards remained in place. One glanced briefly to his left, then forward again.

Nothing unusual.

Everything wrong.

A memory surfaced without permission: the photographs he had studied years later, grainy and compressed, the way violence always seemed smaller after the fact. Bodies arranged awkwardly. Shoes scattered. The world continuing around an event that would later be called a turning point.

He had always hated that phrase. Turning point.
As if history obeyed angles.

A car approached the gate and slowed, then was waved through. Routine held.

He looked again, sharper now, counting positions and distances. He saw it—the deviation. Less than a meter. A man standing where he should not have been.

Seven minutes.

He closed his eyes briefly—not in prayer, but in acceptance.

This was the cost of knowing too much: the realization that stopping one moment did not arrest the next. That the future did not snap obediently back into place.

Somewhere inside the residence, Indira Gandhi was preparing to step outside.

In the version he remembered, she would not live to finish the walk.

He opened his eyes.

History, he understood now, was not waiting to be saved. It was watching to see what he would break instead.
