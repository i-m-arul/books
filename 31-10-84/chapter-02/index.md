Delhi did not announce itself gently.
It pressed in on Arun like a held breath—diesel fumes, dust, the metallic tang of anticipation. The air felt heavier than memory, thicker than imagination. October 1984 was not a place one visited lightly; it was a moment already leaning toward fracture.
He stood at the edge of Safdarjung Road, pretending to study a newspaper headline that screamed optimism it did not believe in. The bold letters spoke of unity, of leadership, of India’s future. Arun folded the paper with deliberate care. He had read the headlines before—years ago, from a safer distance, in history books and documentaries. They had always seemed inevitable.
Now they were simply unfinished.
Indira Gandhi’s residence lay ahead, shielded by manicured hedges and armed certainty. The guards were visible even from here—disciplined silhouettes framed by routine. Routine was the most dangerous thing about this day.
Arun checked his watch.
08:42 a.m.
Less than two hours remained.
The rules—if they could be called that—pressed against his thoughts.
He could not shout a warning.
He could not touch her.
He could not name the date.
But the system—whatever intelligence or accident had pulled him here—had not forbidden influence. Only interference.
A thin distinction. A terrifying one.

The First Shift
Arun walked.
Not toward the gates, but away—down the road, toward a tea stall already gathering its morning regulars. He ordered chai, listening more than speaking. Delhi spoke when allowed.
“Security’s tight today,” one man muttered.
“Always is,” another replied. “But tighter since June.”
June.
Operation Blue Star still rippled through the city like an unhealed wound. Arun felt it in the clipped conversations, the lowered voices. History books summarized events; living cities carried them like bruises.
He paid and moved on.
His target was not Indira Gandhi—not directly.
It was habit.

Routines Are Weaknesses
The Prime Minister’s morning walk was not classified information. It had been written about, romanticized even. The path she took from her residence to her office was as familiar as muscle memory.
And muscle memory could be disrupted.
Arun crossed paths with Ravi Malhotra, a junior officer from the RAW, standing near a parked Ambassador car. Arun recognized him not because Ravi was famous—but because history had forgotten him entirely.
In every timeline Arun had studied, Ravi survived the day and disappeared into anonymity.
That made him useful.
Arun approached casually, holding out a lighter.
“Fire?” he asked.
Ravi obliged, eyes flicking up, assessing, then dismissing Arun as harmless.
“Busy day?” Arun said, as if discussing the weather.
Ravi exhaled smoke. “Every day is.”
Arun nodded, then said softly, “Not like today.”
Ravi stiffened.
Arun did not elaborate. He turned and walked away before suspicion could root itself.
It was not a warning.
It was a disturbance.
A grain of sand.

The Second Shift
By 09:10 a.m., the residence was stirring more noticeably. Arun observed from a distance, noting what had already changed.
A guard repositioned.
A whispered exchange lingered too long.
A radio crackled unnecessarily.
Small things. Unrecorded things.
Arun felt a flicker of hope—and immediately suppressed it.
History resisted edits.
He moved closer now, heart steady, steps unremarkable. The iron gates loomed. The guards were no longer abstractions; they were men with faces, breaths, futures.
And among them—
Satwant Singh.
Beant Singh.
Arun did not look at them directly.
He could not.
Some part of him feared that recognition alone might collapse the fragile probability he was building. Instead, he focused on the space between them—the geometry of their positioning, the angles of trust.
Trust was the real weapon here.
The Moment That Almost Was
At 09:23 a.m., Indira Gandhi stepped into view.
She wore a saffron-colored sari, her posture composed, her expression unreadable. History had always framed her final walk as serene. Arun now understood why.
She looked like someone who had already made peace with consequences—even if she did not know their exact form.
For one impossible second, Arun considered breaking every rule.
Shouting.
Running.
Tearing history open with his hands.
Instead, he did the only thing left.
He dropped the folder.
The thick, government-looking file hit the pavement near the gate with a sharp, deliberate slap.
Heads turned.
Not many.
Just enough.
A senior officer frowned. “What’s that?”
Arun raised his hands apologetically. “Sorry—documents for the PMO. Wrong gate?”
The question was mundane.
The interruption was not.
Routine fractured.

A Delay Is Not a Miracle
Indira Gandhi paused.
Only briefly.
But pauses mattered.
A guard stepped forward to investigate the folder.
Another shifted position to compensate.
The spacing changed.
Arun felt the timeline shudder—not break, but bend.
This was the dangerous part.
History hated being watched while it decided.

The Price of Intervention
As Arun stepped back into the crowd, a sharp pain bloomed behind his eyes. The world flickered—colors oversaturated, sounds lagging by half a second.
He had been warned, though not in words.
Intervention exacted a cost.
He steadied himself against a lamppost, breathing through the nausea, watching as the guards reorganized, as Indira resumed her walk—now flanked differently than before.
The moment was passing.
But it was not the same moment anymore.
Arun did not know yet whether he had succeeded.
Only that the day had changed.
And that history, wounded and alert, was watching him now.
