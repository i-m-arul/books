New Delhi, December 1984

The city had learned to lower its voice.

It wasn’t written anywhere, but everyone felt it—the way conversations paused when a siren passed, the way newspapers lay heavier on breakfast tables, the way grief had curdled into something sharper, more watchful. Indira Gandhi was dead, and India, for the first time in decades, felt leaderless not just politically, but emotionally.

Rajiv Gandhi stood alone in the Prime Minister’s office long after the staff had cleared out. The room still smelled faintly of fresh polish, as though the bureaucracy believed cleanliness could undo history. His mother’s presence lingered everywhere—not as memory, but as architecture. The desk. The files. The silence.

He had not wanted this.

That truth sat inside him like an unacknowledged illness.

Rajiv had been a pilot. He understood machines, controls, altitude, consequence. Politics was different. It was a cockpit where instruments lied and turbulence came from unseen hands. And yet, here he was—thirty-nine years old, sworn in within hours of assassination, elevated not by ambition but by necessity.

Outside, Delhi burned quietly.

Sikh neighborhoods smoldered under curfews and complicity. Reports came in carefully worded phrases: unrest, retaliation, loss of control. No one used the word massacre in official briefings. Words mattered too much now.

Rajiv turned to the window. Lutyens’ Delhi lay spread beneath a pale winter sun, serene and deceptive. Somewhere beyond the geometry of power, trains were stalled, homes were being torched, and the Republic was learning how thin its moral spine could become.

A soft knock interrupted his thoughts.

“Prime Minister,” his principal secretary said gently, stepping inside. “The intelligence summary from the South.”

Rajiv accepted the folder without looking at it. He already knew which state it concerned.

Tamil Nadu.

The South had always watched Delhi differently—less reverence, more calculation. And Tamil Nadu, in particular, had long memories. Memories of language impositions. Of dismissed governments. Of New Delhi’s convenient amnesia when it came to regional pride.

He opened the file.

The first page mentioned Sri Lanka.

The second mentioned Tamil sentiment.

The third mentioned three letters that would soon begin to haunt him: LTTE.

Rajiv exhaled slowly.

Across the Palk Strait, a civil war was sharpening its knives. Ethnic Tamils were being slaughtered in pogroms that mirrored India’s own sins too closely for comfort. Refugees were already arriving on Tamil Nadu’s shores, carrying stories that travelled faster than any intelligence cable.

And where stories went, politics followed.

“Sir,” the secretary said carefully, “the Chief Minister in Madras is asking for a meeting. He wants assurances. Publicly.”

Public assurances were dangerous. They hardened positions. They created expectations that could not be quietly undone.

Rajiv closed the file.

“Tell him we’ll respond,” he said. “Not yet.”

The secretary hesitated. “There is… another note, sir.”

Rajiv looked up.

“The Research & Analysis Wing believes militant elements in Tamil Nadu are becoming… sympathetic. Financial support. Logistics. Nothing conclusive.”

Nothing conclusive was how fractures began.

“Keep monitoring,” Rajiv said. “Discreetly.”

When the door closed again, the silence returned—thicker now, almost accusatory.

Rajiv sat down at the desk and ran his fingers along its edge. This desk had seen wars planned, governments dismissed, enemies forgiven and destroyed in equal measure. His mother had ruled from here with certainty. He ruled from it with inheritance.

Outside this room, the Congress Party was splintering even as it celebrated its sympathy wave victory. Loyalists were circling, smelling permanence. Rivals were waiting, patient and quiet. The party had won an election, but it had lost a compass.

Rajiv understood one thing with unsettling clarity:
Power, once acquired without preparation, demanded payment—with interest.

Madras, early 1985

The sea did not care about borders.

It rose and fell against the shore with mechanical indifference, as it had long before men drew lines across maps and called them nations. On the Marina Beach, fishermen pulled in their nets while police patrols walked just far enough behind to be visible.

Inside a modest government bungalow, a meeting was underway that would never appear in official minutes.

A senior Tamil Nadu bureaucrat spoke first. “Delhi will hesitate. They always do.”

A man in khadi leaned forward. “Delhi hesitates because it doesn’t understand us. Or Sri Lanka.”

Another voice—younger, sharper—cut in. “Understanding is irrelevant. Sentiment is moving faster than policy.”

The room fell quiet.

Across Tamil Nadu, posters had begun to appear—subtle at first. Black ribbons. Slogans invoking shared blood, shared language. Radio conversations carried anger wrapped in poetry. Sympathy for Sri Lankan Tamils was no longer fringe emotion; it was becoming political currency.

And currency always attracted brokers.

“There are whispers,” the younger man continued. “Training camps. Money moving offshore. Delhi won’t like it.”

“Delhi doesn’t need to like it,” the bureaucrat replied. “They need to manage it. Or it will manage them.”

Outside, the sea kept moving.

New Delhi, 1987

History had accelerated.

Rajiv Gandhi now spoke with more confidence, but the cost of learning was evident in the lines etched into his face. The files on his desk had multiplied. Sri Lanka had gone from footnote to fixation.

Advisers argued. Generals briefed. Diplomats warned.

If India did nothing, it would lose influence in its own backyard.
If India intervened, it risked inheriting a war it did not start.

Rajiv listened, absorbing contradictions like a man learning to fly mid-storm.

Finally, he spoke.

“We cannot be spectators,” he said. “If peace is impossible without us, then we step in.”

The room stilled.

The decision would be framed as humanitarian. As regional stability. As moral responsibility.

In truth, it was something else entirely.

It was the first time Rajiv chose action over caution.

Outside the cabinet room, an aide whispered to another, “This will follow him.”

He was right.

---

New Delhi, 1990

The fracture had widened.

Rajiv was no longer Prime Minister, but power had not released him. It rarely did. Enemies remembered. Allies recalculated. The ghosts of decisions made in urgency now demanded acknowledgment.

Tamil Nadu was restless. Sri Lanka was unresolved. Intelligence reports spoke in darker tones now—of betrayal narratives, of vendettas maturing quietly.

Rajiv read one such report late at night.

It ended with a single, chilling line:

Certain fractures, once created, seek symmetry.

He closed the file and stared into the darkness beyond the window.

History, he realized too late, did not repeat itself kindly.
It repeated itself precisely—waiting for the moment when everyone believed the danger had passed.

Somewhere, the machinery of consequence had begun to move.

And it did not require his consent.
