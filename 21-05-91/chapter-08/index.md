By the time the first buses arrived, the ground at Sriperumbudur already looked smaller than it should have.
Volunteers marked boundaries with thin ropes that sagged under their own uncertainty. The stage stood slightly off-center, erected quickly, its wooden planks still smelling of fresh cuts and hurried labor. Loudspeakers crackled as technicians tested them again and again, each burst of static cutting through the early evening heat.
Nothing felt unfinished.
Nothing felt settled either.
People came in clusters — families, factory workers, college students, local party cadres — their conversations overlapping in a low, constant murmur. Some carried small flags. Others held nothing at all, as if unsure whether this was a celebration or an obligation.
Security personnel were present, but not in the way they were supposed to be.
They stood spaced apart, scanning more out of habit than intent. Their eyes followed movement, not individuals. It was a posture born of repetition — of rallies that had ended peacefully for years, of warnings that had always dissolved into nothing.
Someone noticed it first, then another.
Certain faces were missing.
National leaders who would normally occupy the front rows had sent regrets. Some cited scheduling conflicts. Others never explained. A few local Tamil leaders, reliable fixtures at such events, were absent without comment.
The absence created gaps — physical and psychological. Space where authority should have condensed instead thinned.
It went largely unspoken. Absence, after all, is impolite to question.
Rajiv Gandhi arrived without ceremony.
There was no dramatic swell of sound at first, just recognition passing through the crowd like a wave delayed by distance. People leaned forward. A few raised their hands. The chant began unevenly, caught, and then found rhythm.
He smiled easily, almost reflexively, as if smiling required less effort than seriousness. He looked younger than his office demanded, less armored than the moment required.
The rally schedule was already behind.
A microphone failed and had to be replaced. Someone whispered urgently near the stage steps. A senior party worker glanced at his watch and then looked away, as though time could be negotiated if ignored.
Rajiv Gandhi stepped forward anyway.
He did not speak immediately. He waved, nodding in acknowledgment of faces he could not see clearly from the glare of the lights. The distance between him and the crowd felt unusually narrow — not measured in meters, but in intent.
Security adjusted around him, subtly, imperfectly. There was no tight cordon, only a human shape formed by familiarity. He had walked like this before. Many times.
The crowd pressed closer, drawn by the illusion of access.
Someone in the front lifted a garland, bright against the dusk. It hovered briefly above the heads of those around, signaling what was about to happen. A gesture rehearsed across decades of Indian politics.
Rajiv Gandhi noticed it and nodded.
A handler hesitated — just long enough to consider protocol — then stepped aside. The decision was not conscious. It was cultural. Refusal would have felt wrong. Dangerous, even.
The loudspeakers began to squeal again, feedback rising sharply before cutting out. A technician cursed under his breath.
In that moment — insignificant, forgettable — several people felt the same thing without naming it.
A tightening.
Nothing visible.
Nothing actionable.
Just the sense that the air had shifted slightly, like the pause before a train passes too close.
No one spoke it aloud.
The rally had begun.
