The first second passed unnoticed.

Rajiv Gandhi’s hands were still moving, adjusting the garland as it slipped unevenly against his collar. The flowers brushed his chest, light, almost playful, their weight negligible.

In the second second, someone stepped closer than intended.

A security officer registered the movement but did not react. The distance was already gone. Training recognizes threats before they arrive; this one had arrived looking like ritual.

By the third second, the crowd leaned inward.

Bodies compressed. Sound dulled. The loudspeakers, mercifully, were silent.

At four seconds, Rajiv Gandhi bent slightly forward — a reflex learned early, meant to accept garlands without awkwardness. His posture exposed nothing unusual. It had exposed him before without consequence.

At five seconds, a hand moved.

Not suddenly. Not violently. Just enough.

Six.

The device detonated low, close, intimate.

The sound was not a single noise but a tearing — air collapsing inward before exploding outward. Heat followed immediately, then force. The body absorbed what it could not evade.

Seven seconds vanished into debris.

The garland disintegrated. Wood splintered. Metal screamed as it twisted. The ground rose violently to meet those standing on it.

At eight seconds, people were already falling — not yet screaming, not yet running. Shock delayed response. Muscles forgot instruction.

Nine.

Rajiv Gandhi was no longer upright. His body had been thrown backward, then down, then still. The motion completed itself before comprehension could begin.

At ten seconds, security reacted.

Weapons were drawn without targets. Orders were shouted without clarity. Someone yelled his name — once — as if volume could reverse physics.

Eleven seconds carried the first screams.

High, sharp, uncontrolled. Panic found its voice.

At twelve seconds, smoke thickened, acrid and metallic. Visibility collapsed. People stumbled blindly, hands outstretched, colliding with each other, with bodies, with parts of the stage now unrecognizable.

Thirteen seconds ended.

The journalist had not moved.

He stood several meters away, notebook still in his hand, pen hovering uselessly above paper that would never record this moment accurately. His mind searched instinctively for framing — blast, attack, incident — and found none sufficient.

He understood something then, with a clarity that would never leave him.

This was not chaos.

It was consequence.

Every warning softened.
Every absence ignored.
Every hesitation justified.

They had not failed to stop history.

They had made room for it.

As smoke drifted and the first bodies were lifted, the journalist finally lowered his pen.

History didn’t need permission.
It only needed space.
