Morning arrived without ceremony.

It always did.

The air was already warm when Rajiv Gandhi opened his eyes, the ceiling fan tracing its familiar circle above him like a thought that refused to settle. Somewhere nearby, a door closed softly. A voice murmured instructions. The day assembled itself around him with quiet efficiency.

Another rally. Another crowd. Another promise of connection.

He sat up slowly, letting the weight of sleep lift in stages. Campaign days had a rhythm that no longer surprised him. Early briefings. Travel. Smiles held longer than comfort demanded. Conversations interrupted before they could reach depth.

And yet, this morning felt lighter.

Not hopeful. Just… unburdened.

He dressed without hurry. Chose simplicity over symbolism. When an aide asked whether he wanted to review the final schedule again, he waved it away.

“I know where I’m going,” he said.

He paused, then added, almost to himself, “Seems like I’m campaigning alone these days.”

The aide smiled, unsure whether it was humor or observation.

---

In a modest guesthouse not far away, the journalist sat on the edge of the bed, notebook open but untouched.

He had woken earlier than usual, startled not by a sound but by the sudden absence of it. The kind of silence that follows a question you didn’t know you were asking.

He checked his watch.

Plenty of time.

Still, he did not lie back down.

He washed his face, watching the water disappear down the drain, and tried to name the restlessness in his chest. It was not fear. Fear had a shape. This did not.

He told himself it was anticipation.

Big events carried weight. They pulled at the nervous system.

That was all.

He closed the notebook and stood.

---

Across town, the Tamil leader who had stayed away woke late.

The decision not to attend had granted him the luxury of an unstructured morning. No convoy. No briefing. No performance.

He drank his coffee slowly, flipping through the newspaper without absorbing its words.

At one point, he lingered on a photograph from a different rally—Rajiv smiling, leaning forward, accepting a garland from a woman who looked both proud and overwhelmed.

The image unsettled him.

Not because of danger.

Because of intimacy.

He folded the paper and pushed it aside.

---

At the venue, preparations resumed with renewed energy.

Morning light revealed what artificial illumination had softened the night before. Colors sharpened. Distances became more honest.

Volunteers took positions. Police adjusted their stances. Barricades were nudged into alignment by hands that had done this countless times.

Routine settled in.

The SPG officer walked the front line again, slower this time.

Crowd estimates had been revised upward. Not dramatically—just enough to matter.

He made a small adjustment to his position, then another. His body moved before thought, responding to pressure he sensed rather than measured.

Everything remained within acceptable bounds.

He radioed in a brief update.

No flags.

---

Rajiv Gandhi reviewed a short note handed to him over breakfast.

Local turnout was high.

The crowd was enthusiastic.

Arrangements were smooth.

He nodded, satisfied.

Tamil Nadu had always been complicated, but it had also been generous. Emotional. Expressive.

He preferred that to indifference.

He thought briefly of Sonia, of the children. Campaigns kept him moving, but they also reminded him why movement mattered.

He stood, ready.

---

The journalist arrived at the rally grounds earlier than planned.

The place was already alive. People gathered in clusters, talking over one another, laughing, pointing toward the stage as if proximity alone conferred participation.

He walked the perimeter instinctively, counting nothing, measuring nothing, simply letting the scene register.

There it was again.

That sense of compression.

Not physical. Temporal.

As if the day were moving faster than it should.

He stopped near a group of women holding garlands wrapped carefully in cloth.

They spoke excitedly about how close they hoped to get.

“Just a moment,” one said. “Just to see him properly.”

The phrase stayed with him.

Just a moment.

---

The police superintendent took a call.

Nothing urgent. A clarification about parking. A request for an additional volunteer at the far entrance.

He handled it efficiently.

He told himself, as he often did, that control was not about force. It was about flow.

Flow looked good today.

---

Rajiv Gandhi’s convoy departed.

As the vehicles moved, something subtle shifted—not in the plan, but in the atmosphere.

Expectation rippled outward from the road, reaching the crowd before the sound of engines did. People adjusted their positions unconsciously, tightening their grip on garlands, leaning forward, narrowing space.

The journalist felt it too.

He glanced at his watch again.

Time felt different now.

Not faster. Heavier.

---

At home, the Tamil leader turned on the television.

Just briefly.

Live coverage had not yet begun, but the anchors were already speaking with heightened energy.

He watched for a minute, then turned it off.

He did not want the day mediated.

---

At the venue, radios crackled more frequently.

Rajiv Gandhi’s arrival was imminent.

The SPG officer took his final position.

He scanned faces, movements, gestures.

Nothing stood out. And yet, he felt the need to remember this moment. He did not know why.

---

The journalist moved closer to the front.

Not because he intended to. Because the crowd carried him.

He let it happen.

Sometimes, understanding required surrender.

---

When the convoy turned into the final stretch, a cheer rose instinctively, premature and unstoppable.

The rally had not yet begun. But something had already locked into place.

A sequence had started.

And like most sequences, it did not announce its end.
