Years later, the ground at Sriperumbudur looks ordinary.

Traffic passes without pause. Vendors sell tea where crowds once stood. The place has been folded back into geography, its significance reduced to a signboard and an occasional wreath on a predictable date.

History prefers locations that behave.

Across the country, security protocols have thickened. Barricades are heavier. Distances are measured more carefully. Lists are longer. Warnings are documented, archived, escalated.

And yet.

Elsewhere, leaders still step closer than advised. Crowds still press forward. Ritual still overrides caution. Absence still creates space without announcing itself.

Inquiries have been conducted. Reports written. Lessons summarized. Each ends with reassurance — this will not happen again — as if history respects vows.

But fractures do not announce themselves as repetitions. They appear as familiarity.

A pause before intervention.
A warning softened by context.
A moment dismissed because nothing happened last time.

The journalist, older now, no longer covers politics. He notices patterns anyway — in different countries, different languages, different decades. The shape is always recognizable. Only the names change.

He understands what most prefer not to.

History does not repeat itself because it wants to.
It repeats because it is allowed to.

Not through conspiracy.
Not through inevitability.

Through space.

And somewhere, even now, a space is being made.
